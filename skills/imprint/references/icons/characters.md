# Character construction for application icons

Read when a creature, person, mascot, or personified object carries the icon's identity, whether flat, painted, dimensional, or pixel. A merely recognizable animal/object without character treatment may use only its rendering guide. Pair this subject guide with the renderer selected in [direction](direction.md); return to the [workflow](workflow.md) for choices and delivery. Character logos have their separate Logo route.

## Character brief

Translate the intended feeling and reference cues into an attitude, dominant silhouette, identifying feature, face or gesture, and framing. Product fit can be emotional or behavioral; an app's function need not appear as a tiny prop. An abstract creature is viable when its distinguishing contour or behavior is visible.

Treat subject, complexity, line treatment, shading, and crop as separate decisions. “Animal,” “minimal,” “outlined,” “soft gradient,” and “peeking” can describe one character together. Facial features and anatomical completeness are optional; posture or a distinctive contour may carry personality. Cute proportions serve a chosen brief, not every character.

## Silhouette, expression, and pose

Establish one dominant mass and the few features that make this particular character recognizable. Compare silhouette, body/head proportions, posture, and identifying asymmetry before adding ornament. Recoloring a generic round body with two eyes is not concept variety.

Design facial grammar deliberately: eye spacing, size and tilt, gaze when present, and mouth/brow only when useful. Match expression to the intended attitude, including quiet, curious, mischievous, or confident. Check that the same expression survives reduction; replace fragile detail with clearer proportions when necessary.

For a held object, contact, or interaction, make the relationship unambiguous in the dominant masses. Prefer a readable pose over several explanatory props. Preserve essential separation between limbs, ears, features, and nearby shapes.

## Framing

Choose whole figure, head, or close-up for the tile. Tilt and asymmetry still need an optical center. A deliberate crop can strengthen engagement; retain the features needed for recognition under the target mask. Protect ears, tufts, gaze, and gestures as the design requires, allowing intentional profile or occlusion.

Establish figure/background contrast through broad color regions. Enlarge the face or change the crop when a delicate expression fails at icon size. Do not try to fix that failure by adding texture.

## Preserve character across rendering methods

| Rendering | What must survive |
| --- | --- |
| Graphic/cartoon | Facial spacing, drawn silhouette, contour hierarchy, and coherent color regions; flat, cel-shaded, and soft-gradient treatments are all possible |
| Illustration | Expression and identifying contour beneath brush, print, or paper language |
| Dimensional | Face and pose after perspective, material, lighting, and occlusion; fiber or reflections must not replace facial structure |
| Pixel | Identifying feature and attitude expressed through deliberate clusters; omit details the grid cannot support |

Add subject, attitude, distinctive feature, facial relationships, pose, crop, and protected features to the workflow's render brief. Preserve reference simplification and depth cues when requested; distinguish the edit target from style references. A style reference alone does not request copying its specific character identity or surrounding presentation.

## Inspect the character

Test silhouette, expression, feature separation, and intended-mask behavior before showing candidates. Inspect 64 and 32 px studies during exploration; full delivery follows the workflow's complete size and target checks. If the character feels interchangeable, revisit contour, attitude, and gesture. If the intended drawn treatment looks like toy photography, revisit rendering. If it reads only at full size, revisit feature proportions and framing.
