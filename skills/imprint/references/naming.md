# Naming directions and candidate evaluation

Read for every naming request, including direction exploration, shortlist-only work, and final-name selection. Use the Identity Brief and Brand Strategy, [User choices](../SKILL.md#user-choices), and [conflict screening](screening.md). An accepted name remains fixed unless revision is authorized; screening it does not require inventing alternatives.

## Discover naming preferences

Establish what is being named: company, creative practice/brand, product, or application, and any relationship to existing names. Reuse the user's desired feeling and fixed constraints. Ask only about consequential gaps: naming languages and markets; direct description versus suggestive imagery or sound; desired tone; reading/spelling expectations; length preferences; and liked or disliked examples with reasons. For multiple languages, establish whether the user wants alternative names or a paired identity, and whether meaning, sound, or independent naturalness should connect the pair. Treat these as decision dimensions, not a mandatory questionnaire.

When direction is open, offer a few brief-specific options with their intended impression, connection, and main trade-off. Explain choices in ordinary language: direct and readily understood, evocative and image-led, or invented and sound-led may be useful contrasts, but are not a fixed menu. A small illustrative word or phrase can make a direction concrete; label it as an unscreened illustration rather than a recommended or available name. The word-formation methods below are construction tools, not substitutes for asking how the identity should feel.

Apply User choices to establish the chosen direction or authorized comparison before the candidate pool. An explicit request for a varied shortlist already authorizes exploring multiple directions; keep its relevant preferences and label each candidate's direction instead of demanding an extra territory decision. When a user says they have no ideas, help them compare options; a recommendation remains a proposal until chosen or delegated.

Complete preference discovery when the record identifies the naming subject, relevant language/usage constraints, desired feeling, and chosen or authorized trial directions. For naming-plus-visual work, the name's tone informs the family; it does not predetermine a literal logo subject or icon style.

## Establish Naming Territories before candidates

Establish a few distinct territories before generating candidate names. Record each territory's core idea, product/strategy connection, intended perception, advantages, and risks. Engineering metaphor, function, concept, science/mathematics, culture/history, product behavior, abstraction, and linguistic invention are possible sources rather than a mandatory menu.

Use brief facts → intended experience, value, or character → naming territory. Separate established capabilities from aspirations. Decide where the connection belongs: an explicit use, a desired feeling, a recognizable character, or an indirect image or metaphor. The company, practice, or product constrains the fit; its nouns and verbs need not supply the name's vocabulary.

For an open or metaphorical brief, explore the brand idea before collecting product keywords; make functional description the organizing principle only when the brief requires it. Identify a meaningful behavior, relationship, or tension in the experience, then look for analogous situations, objects, living things, roles, or cultural images outside the product category. For example, permission to experiment can connect to rehearsal: trying, revising, and trying again before a larger commitment. This illustrates a relationship, not a prescribed word source or candidate.

For each direction, keep one specific connection and the intended impression. A metaphor need not disclose the function or be decoded by a first-time user. A short explanation may reveal a coherent second layer; it should not be required to make an awkward name appealing. Depth comes from a fitting relationship, not obscure vocabulary, ancient roots, or generic claims such as innovation and limitless possibility. A direction may also work through personality or sound without a deep allegory. If a word or sound occurs during exploration, hold it provisionally until territories are established; test its fit rather than inventing a meaning to justify it.

Compare directions by their image, attitude, or relationship, not merely their word endings. A shared product keyword with interchangeable companions is one family, even if the spellings differ. When the shortlist calls for distinct directions, broaden the underlying associations before adding more variants; changing from a compound to a phrase alone does not create a new direction. Follow an indirect brief without reserving slots for descriptive names merely to cover a category.

## Gather material and vary formation

Choose sources relevant to the direction: objects users handle, actions, desired states, real-world analogues, working structures, helpful roles, nature, culture, or sound and letter patterns. These are avenues for exploration, not required categories or pre-cleared names. Ancient languages and mythology are optional sources, with no presumption of higher quality.

For promising directions, try more than one suitable way of forming words before comparing the results. These methods can overlap; there is no full matrix or per-method quota, and no default requirement for a functional compound, short invented word, or fashionable suffix.

| Formation | What to explore | Main trade-off to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing word, including a borrowed word | Adopt a word with a useful meaning, image, or sound | Familiarity in the target languages, existing uses, search ambiguity |
| Whole-word compound | Combine words through a meaningful or expressive relationship | Natural combination, length, and contribution of each part |
| Blend | Compress or overlap word fragments | Pronunciation, spelling, and whether the result works without decoding it |
| Spelling variant | Modify a familiar written form | Repeated oral spelling corrections or confusion with the original |
| Root and affix | Use a productive prefix or suffix | Grammatical and spoken plausibility, formulaic similarity |
| Short phrase | Express an attitude, action, or memorable scene | Phrase rhythm, word-order recall, length in real use |
| Sound-led coinage | Develop a pronounceable form without a required literal meaning | First impression and learning cost; do not attach invented etymology |

For combinations, identify the relationship: object/action, function/role, modifier/image, complementary ideas, or deliberate contrast. Ask what each added element contributes: meaning, imagery, attitude, rhythm, humor, or merely availability. Sound or surprise can justify an element without a deep product allegory. If it contributes little, remove or replace it. Judge the resulting name independently of the derivation; a clever recipe can yield an awkward word.

Avoid meaningless AI additions, fashionable -ly/-ify/-io endings, mechanical technology-word pairings, recycled Cogn/Syn/Nex/Neural roots, hard-to-spell futurism, and spelling distorted just for a domain. These are risks to inspect in context, not proof that every name sharing a form is poor. Favor a few strong, strategically different candidates over a long public list.

## Separate origin, association, and brand intent

- **Origin:** Historical meaning, language, and derivation are factual claims. Verify them using reliable linguistic sources; a brand's own account establishes its stated naming intent, not linguistic universality.
- **Association:** What today's audience might infer is a hypothesis until researched. Familiar roots do not guarantee that users recover a compound's intended meaning.
- **Brand intent:** A designed interpretation is legitimate when labelled as such. Describe altered roots as an inspired coinage and identify the alteration; do not present them as an authentic ancient-language word.

The name should remain acceptable when its origin story is removed. A story can enrich identity, but cannot repair a reading or impression problem. A sound-led name may be semantically opaque and still work; opacity is not itself failure.

## Evaluate communication before external screening

First inspect names alone, then in short sentences suited to the product, such as “We use ___” or “I tested the idea with ___.” Consider the brief's priority languages and markets, not an undefined worldwide audience.

- **Seeing and saying:** Plausible readings, stress ambiguity, awkward sound sequences, and unintended associations.
- **Hearing and typing:** Likely spellings, recurring corrections, omitted letters, and confusion with nearby words or brands.
- **Remembering and finding:** Whether the name has a distinct hook, could collapse into a common alternative, or invites search correction. Use actual search evidence for search-behavior claims.
- **Visual and brand potential:** Whether the written form has a usable wordmark rhythm and distinctive character, whether the tone matches the traits/anti-traits, and whether the name can remain useful beyond a short-lived category trend. These are design judgments until tested.
- **Product fit and extension:** Intended character, coherence of the chosen association, and room for plausible adjacent features. Judge fit at the level chosen in the brief, rather than demanding that a metaphor also explain the workflow. An accurate feature label may still feel generic.

Separate unfamiliar meaning from a communication problem. For an indirect name, needing a product descriptor or not revealing the category is not itself a weakness. Identify the actual trade-off: an awkward reading, a misleading promise, an unwanted association, or the effort required to establish recognition. In the names-alone comparison, ask whether the name has an appealing sound, a distinct character, and a memorable hook; recovering its intended symbolism is not a comprehension test. Apply the same distinction when reviewing or benchmarking naming output.

Treat length and syllable preferences as brief constraints or exploratory aids, not universal quality rules. Compare clarity, distinction, character, and usability through concrete strengths and costs. A design judgment does not replace factual screening or audience testing.

Before external screening, record each candidate's concrete strength, main concern, and disposition: screen, retain for comparison, or retire for quality. For a quality retirement, identify the defect and why it is substantial for this brief; distinguish an explicit constraint violation or observed problem from a model's aesthetic or communication judgment. A possible alternate spelling, uncertain association, or unfamiliar meaning alone is a predicted risk, not a demonstrated failure. Keep otherwise promising candidates with such concerns available for comparison, noting what evidence or audience test could resolve the concern. Prioritize the strongest candidates within the screening budget; retaining a candidate internally does not qualify it for the final shortlist or require testing every possibility.

Model judgments are not native-speaker approval, recall tests, or global language clearance. Clearly distinguish checked facts, predicted risks, and untested behavior. If audience testing is requested, use names without leading stories for read-aloud, heard-spelling, delayed-recall, and association tasks, and report actual observations. Otherwise mark those checks unperformed rather than simulating human results.

## Recheck quality after external screening

Before shortlisting, compare each survivor with its pre-search quality assessment. Judge the name alone and in the same usage sentences: would its strengths still justify recommending it if the other candidates' eliminations were unknown? Record the quality decision, its concrete reason, and any remaining communication uncertainty separately from the conflict-screening outcome. Change the earlier quality judgment only for a stated reason, such as new linguistic evidence or a clearer comparison; sparse search results and being the last survivor do not supply naming quality. A recommendation may have trade-offs, provided its strengths justify them for the brief.

Evaluate each credible candidate without its origin story, then with the product context. Resolve material wording, fit, or communication defects before shortlisting; inspect revised names again in the same usage situations.

Shortlist candidates supported by naming quality, with explicit external screening status. When screening is unavailable or outside the agreed task, a design shortlist may be delivered as availability-unverified; do not describe it as screened or final availability validation. Present normally 3–5 names unless the user specifies otherwise, each traceable to a territory. When replacing rejected candidates, try another formation if the territory is strong or broaden the underlying association if it is exhausted. Respect the main skill's resource limit; disclose an incomplete shortlist rather than promoting deferred candidates to fill it. Selection follows the user's retained or delegated authority. Deepen screening of the selected name before claiming completed screening; if unavailable or excluded, preserve that limitation in the selected-name record.

## Final choice and handoff

For a final-name task, present the reviewed shortlist with exact spelling, pronunciation guidance, intended impression, strengths, trade-offs, and actual screening status. Identify the recommendation and apply User choices to obtain selection of the exact name; an earlier territory preference does not select a candidate. Complete applicable checks before presenting it for selection, then deepen screening of the selected name within scope. If new evidence requires a replacement or material spelling change, present that decision to the user under the same authority rules.

A shortlist-only task completes when its requested comparison and evidence status are delivered. A final-name task also requires a selected name and its usage record. While selection is pending, keep candidates labelled as options rather than treating the recommendation as the final brand or developing dependent final lettering. A user's acceptance establishes preference, not availability or linguistic verification.

Record the selected name's canonical spelling, capitalization, spacing, pronunciation guidance, meaning/origin, territory, and selection rationale, distinguishing its written form from any agreed wordmark styling. Label pronunciation recommendations and coined meanings as design choices where appropriate. Add guidance on abbreviations, derivative names, or character voice only when relevant to the requested use; preserve established conventions for an existing brand. Keep this compact in the selected-name record rather than expanding a naming task into a full brand system.

Treat the naming metaphor as a conceptual anchor, with its use in imagery, copy, and character voice decided for the brief. An animal or character in a name does not by itself call for puns, mascot narration, or a family of names built from its parts. Judge extensions by the identity of the whole name and the intended experience, rather than requiring every component to describe every new feature literally.

## Illustrative cases

Consult these only when an example helps distinguish methods. They establish reported naming choices, not a formula for commercial success or templates to copy.

- [Buffer's founder](https://buffer.com/resources/how-to-name-your-startup/) describes choosing a real word related to the service.
- [Mailchimp's official guide](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/writing-about-mailchimp/) describes a mail-delivering chimp metaphor and separately specifies the name's capitalization, prohibits splitting it into new names, and excludes writing in its mascot's voice. These are that brand's usage decisions, not universal restrictions on character-based names.
- [flomo's official explanation](https://help.flomo.app/about-us) identifies flow and memo as its source words. This documents a blend's stated origin; the page provides no pronunciation, recall, or audience-comprehension testing.

Keep the exploration record concise rather than turning every candidate into a long origin story.
