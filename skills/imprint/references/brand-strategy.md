# Identity brief and brand strategy

Read when establishing the Identity Brief and Brand Strategy for a company, creative brand/practice, product, or application. Maintain a compact shared record rather than a long discovery report. Later references use this record as the source of strategy; do not rewrite a different personality for each asset.

## Identity Brief

Capture:

- Identity subject and category: what is being named or represented, what exists now, and what is only planned. Distinguish a company or creator's umbrella brand from an individual product or app; establish whether their names and identities should be shared or separate when both are in scope.
- Purpose and core value: the offering, practice, or experience, its value to the audience, and its distinctive approach when known. A company or creative practice need not have an app or a single software feature.
- People and situations: primary users, priority languages/markets, and usage contexts.
- Competitive context: known alternatives and category expectations; label user-supplied claims and assumptions. Research consequential unknowns with the evidence rules in [screening](screening.md).
- Desired impression and preferences: how the identity should feel, liked/disliked names or visuals and why, relevant references, and impressions to avoid. Discover shared preferences once, then use each requested route's guidance to resolve its open choices under [User choices](../SKILL.md#user-choices).
- Scope and constraints: naming, logo, app icon, existing decisions, target media, and budget.

Ask when an unknown changes direction, not to complete a form. An exploratory brief may carry bounded assumptions; distinguish that from a confirmed positioning decision. Stop before generating candidate names or forms.

## Brand Strategy

Write a concise positioning statement: for whom, in which situation, the company, practice, or product offers what value, and why its approach matters. Avoid claiming market differentiation without evidence.

Choose 3–5 traits with real consequences from the brief and user preferences. For each record the intended behavior, a naming implication, a visual implication, and an anti-trait that distinguishes a nearby wrong direction. For example, “precise” might mean controlled naming rhythm and deliberate alignment, with “sterile” as a risk to avoid; it does not mandate a geometric symbol. Select traits for this identity, not from a fixed preferred aesthetic. Distinguish user preferences from agent hypotheses; discuss consequential tensions rather than silently replacing either the brief or the user's taste.

Define the brand personality in one short paragraph and the relevant anti-patterns. “Modern,” “premium,” “innovative,” and “beautiful” alone do not constrain decisions. Explain the visible or verbal choices meant by such words when they are useful.

Record one or two plausible future extensions. Use them to test whether a name or mark is unnecessarily tied to the first feature; do not invent a corporate roadmap.

## Decision trace

For each major candidate, keep a compact chain:

`Brief fact or bounded assumption → brand intention → naming/visual decision → visible or verbal evidence → trade-off`

Traceability means coherent fit, not that a stranger must decode the product from the logo. Metaphor, character, emotion, and sound can express the strategy. The explanation must not supply a feature absent from the actual artwork or make an awkward name acceptable.

The working record should distinguish fixed decisions from provisional hypotheses and retain candidate IDs through iterations. Record stage outcomes as ready, revise, unresolved, or outside scope, with a reason and artifact/evidence links. Separately record direction/concept/final choices, exact selected versions, and user choice or explicit delegation; a ready artifact may still await selection. Keep the record concise and tie each quality judgment to the current wording or artwork.
